Do You Need a Permit to Replace
Windows in San Francisco?

July 7, 2026 7 min read By SF Window Station

Short answer: yes, almost always. San Francisco requires a building permit for window replacement in nearly every case, and if your home is a Victorian or Edwardian on a street-facing facade, the rules get stricter. The good news is that the process is far less painful than most homeowners fear, and a good contractor handles all of it for you.

Here is how it actually works in 2026, based on the projects we permit and install across the city every week.

When a Permit Is Required

The San Francisco Department of Building Inspection (DBI) requires a permit for window replacement when any of the following is true, which covers almost every real project:

  • You are changing the window's size, shape, or location in the wall
  • You are changing the frame material (for example, wood to vinyl)
  • You are changing the operation type (double-hung to slider, fixed to casement)
  • The window is on a street-facing facade
  • The building is in a historic or conservation district, or is a designated landmark

Even a true like-for-like replacement (same size, same material, same operation) technically requires an over-the-counter permit in San Francisco. Some contractors will suggest skipping it. Don't: unpermitted window work surfaces during resale disclosures and can force you to redo the job, or retroactively permit it at roughly double the cost and hassle.

The Historic Home Rules Everyone Asks About

San Francisco has more surviving Victorians and Edwardians than any city in America, and the Planning Department protects how they look from the street. If your home is in a designated historic district (parts of Alamo Square, Liberty Hill, Dogpatch, Bush Street, and others) or is individually listed, street-facing windows must match the original appearance in profile, proportion, and often material.

In practice, this means:

  • Front facade: wood or approved wood-clad windows with period-correct profiles; true or simulated divided lites where original. Vinyl is generally not approved facing the street.
  • Sides and rear: far more flexibility: most homeowners use vinyl or fiberglass here and save significantly.
  • Not sure if you're in a district? Look up your address in the SF Property Information Map (sfplanninggis.org), it flags historic status in seconds.

We covered the window styles and materials that pass design review in our guide to the best windows for San Francisco Victorians.

Title 24: The Energy Rule That Shapes What You Can Buy

California's Title 24 energy code applies to every window replacement in the state. In the Bay Area's climate zone, replacement windows must meet a maximum U-factor of 0.30, which in practice means dual-pane, Low-E glass at minimum. Any window we sell meets this out of the box, but it matters if you're comparing quotes: a suspiciously cheap bid sometimes hides non-compliant product that will fail inspection.

What the Inspection Looks Like

After installation, a DBI inspector visits to verify the work matches the permit: correct product, proper flashing and weatherproofing, tempered glass where required (near doors, in bathrooms, close to the floor), and egress compliance in bedrooms. The visit typically takes 15–30 minutes. When we run the project, we schedule the inspection, meet the inspector, and close out the permit. The homeowner usually doesn't need to do anything.

The Bottom Line

Permits add a small, predictable cost and (outside historic districts) very little time. What they buy you is real: legal work that survives resale, insurance validity, and an independent check on installation quality. Any established SF installer prices and handles this routinely, so the simplest test of a contractor is to ask: "Is the permit included, and who meets the inspector?" The right answer to both is "we do."
